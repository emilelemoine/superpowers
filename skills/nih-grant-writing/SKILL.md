# NIH Grant Writing

Use with the `superpowers:scientific-writing-style` skill — every rule there applies here. This document adds what is specific to NIH applications and overrides the general skill on conflicts.

Policy details below are current as of July 2026. **NOFO instructions always supersede this document.** When a fact here matters to a decision, verify it against the NOFO and the NIH Guide Notice before relying on it.

---

## 0. The constraint that governs everything else

**NOT-OD-25-132** (effective for receipt dates on or after 25 September 2025): NIH will not consider an application substantially developed by AI, in whole or in part, to be original. Detection after award can trigger referral for research misconduct, disallowed costs, or termination. NIH also caps a PD/PI at **six new, renewal, resubmission, or revision applications per calendar year**, across all activity codes except T-series and R13 conference grants.

The notice allows that "AI tools may be appropriate to assist in application preparation for limited aspects or in specific circumstances." NIH has since issued further guidance on AI use and disclosure — **check the current notice before each submission cycle**, since this area is moving.

So: your job on a grant is editing, structuring, critiquing, tightening, checking, and drafting *from my content* — never generating the science. If I ask you to write a section from nothing, say so and ask for my material first. Every claim in a draft must trace to something I gave you or to a source you can cite. Never fabricate preliminary data, citations, or aims.

## 1. Review mechanics — write to the actual scorecard

### Research project grants (R01, R21, R03, R15, R33, R34, R61, U01, DP-series and related)

Under the **Simplified Review Framework** (NOT-OD-24-010, due dates on/after 25 January 2025):

| Factor | Contains | Scored? |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Importance of the Research** — "Should it be done?" | Significance, Innovation | 1–9 |
| **2. Rigor and Feasibility** — "Can it be done well?" | Approach | 1–9 |
| **3. Expertise and Resources** — "Are they in place?" | Investigator, Environment | Rating only: *appropriate* or *additional expertise/resources needed* |

Consequences for drafting:

- **Innovation is no longer scored on its own.** It now lives inside the importance argument. Do not give innovation a standalone rhetorical showcase; make it a reason the work matters.
- **Approach carries its own score.** Weak methods can no longer hide behind an exciting question. Rigor detail is now half the scored content.
- **Overall Impact is a separate 1–9 score, not an average.** Reviewers weigh all three factors, and NIH states explicitly that "an application does not need to be strong in all categories to be judged likely to have major scientific impact." In practice, weakness in Factor 1 is the hardest to survive: reviewers who do not believe the work matters rarely champion it. Argue importance first and hardest.
- Foreign organization justification, Select Agents, and Resource Sharing Plans are no longer reviewer-evaluated. Authentication of Key Resources and Budget are reviewed but unscored and cannot affect the impact score.

**The SRF has not been extended to K, F, or T awards.** Do not write a K application to the three-factor framework.

### Career development (K) awards

Still reviewed under **five scored criteria**: Candidate; Career Development Plan / Career Goals and Objectives; Research Plan; Mentor(s), Co-Mentor(s), Consultant(s), Collaborator(s); Environment and Institutional Commitment. Mentored K awards require **9 person-months (75% full-time professional effort)**.

### Fellowships (F)

Three scored criteria since 25 January 2025 (NOT-OD-24-107): Candidate's Preparedness and Potential; Research Training Plan; Commitment to Candidate, Mentoring and Training Environment. Grades are no longer required. (Note the attachment is named "Candidate's Goals, Preparedness, and Potential" — the criterion and the attachment are not identically titled.)

### Scale and process

Whole numbers 1–9; 1 = exceptionally strong with essentially no weaknesses, 9 = serious weaknesses. Reviewers weigh the *importance* of strengths and weaknesses, not their number. NIH has been consolidating review of F, K, and T applications in CSR under recurring activity-code-specific Special Emphasis Panels rather than institute-run panels — check the CSR study section roster for your mechanism, since this changes who reads the application, not the criteria.

## 2. Page limits

| Attachment | Limit |
|---|---|
| Specific Aims (all mechanisms) | **1 page** |
| Research Strategy — R01, R33, U01, R15, and most | **12 pages** |
| Research Strategy — R03, R21, R41, R43, SC2, SC3 | **6 pages** |
| K: "Candidate Information and Goals for Career Development" **+** Research Strategy | **12 pages combined** |
| K: Mentor/Co-Mentor Plans and Statements | 6 pages |
| K: Institutional Environment / Institutional Commitment | 1 page each |
| F: Candidate's Goals, Preparedness and Potential | 3 pages |
| F: Training Activities and Timelines | 3 pages |
| F: Research Training Project Strategy | 6 pages |
| Project Summary / Abstract | **30 lines** |
| Project Narrative | **3 sentences** |
| Introduction to Resubmission | 1 page (3 for training) |
| Training in Responsible Conduct of Research | 1 page |

Biosketch and Other Support now use the **Common Forms** via SciENcv (NOT-OD-26-018, required for due dates on/after 25 January 2026). ORCID iD is mandatory. No page limit, but the Personal Statement is capped at **3,500 characters** and belongs in the NIH Supplement.

The Data Management and Sharing Plan was streamlined to seven elements (NOT-OD-26-046, due dates on/after 25 May 2026): Element 4 (justifying sharing limits) ≤ 300 words; Element 6 (data types and repositories table) ≤ 100 words.

The K "12 pages combined" split is a strategic choice, not an administrative one. A common failure mode is spending 9 pages on career development and 3 on science. Reviewers scrutinize the research plan closely.

## 3. The Specific Aims page

The most important page in the application. A proposal is rarely funded *because* of the aims page, but is often rejected because of it. It is science and sales at once. The audience is an educated non-expert. The goal is not to impress with complexity — it is to recruit one reviewer as an advocate who will argue for funding at the expense of other good proposals.

Four paragraphs.

### Paragraph 1 — Problem, importance, knowledge gap

Convince the reviewer that an important, unsolved problem exists *now*.

- Open with a specific, compelling, immediately informative sentence. The first sentence can open or close the door.
- Summarize current knowledge briefly and accurately.
- Name the gap explicitly. Signal it in words: "a gap remains," "no one knows why," "the critical barrier to progress is."
- Align with the mission and priorities of the institute you are targeting.

Red flags: a generic opening; excessive background or jargon; a gap that is implied but never stated; ignoring a live controversy in the field.

### Paragraph 2 — Rationale, solution, and why this team

- Propose a clear solution to the stated gap and give the explicit rationale for choosing this approach.
- State the overall objective in one realistic sentence.
- State the central hypothesis — falsifiable, and testable with the methods proposed.
- Position the investigator, team, and environment credibly. Specific evidence, not adjectives.
- Establish that **any outcome advances the field**. A proposal whose value depends on one result going one way is a risk reviewers will price in.

Red flags: overpromising; a hypothesis the methods cannot test; vague claims of expertise; framing the objective as "to test a hypothesis" rather than as knowledge to be gained.

### Paragraph 3 — The aims

- **Two to four aims; three is ideal.**
- Aims are **independent**. No aim may depend on another succeeding. State this explicitly if it is not obvious.
- Each aim opens with a concrete, active verb: *measure, estimate, determine, test, quantify, compare*. Not *describe, explore, investigate, correlate, characterize*.
- Each aim should be capable of producing a standalone manuscript.
- Aims flow logically and tell one coherent story; each addresses the stated gap.
- **No new concept or term appears for the first time in an aim.** Everything an aim relies on has been introduced above it.
- Give each aim a working hypothesis and one or two sentences of approach and expected outcome.

Strongly discouraged: "exploratory" aims; sub-aims proliferating as 1a/1b/1c; aim dependency.

### Paragraph 4 — Impact and payoff

- State expected outcomes concretely.
- Articulate the innovation — conceptual, methodological, or both — as a reason the work matters, not as a boast.
- State the impact on the field, on practice, or on science, tied to the specific aims.
- Make the return on investment to the funding agency explicit.
- Close the loop with the opening problem. Suggest the logical next study where appropriate.

Red flags: generic claims of importance; impact untethered from the aims; ending without returning to the central problem.

## 4. Grant prose differs from paper prose

- **Front-load.** Use ABDCE, not OCAR. Hoist the challenge into the opening as the initial action, then fill in background. Reviewers read on airplanes when they would rather sleep. They are not patient.
- **"If you haven't told them in the first two pages, you haven't told them."** The whole story — problem, question, plan, payoff — lands in the first two pages or it does not land.
- **Fuzzy hypotheses guarantee a "do not fund."** A hypothesis that covers every possibility cannot be wrong, so it cannot be tested. Weak: "Microbial community composition is controlled by plant inputs, water availability, and soil chemistry." Strong: "The chemical nature of plant inputs is *the single strongest control* on soil microbial community composition." Name one factor as dominant. Give the reviewer something to disagree with.
- **Challenging proposals sometimes get funded; boring ones never do.**
- **Protect the resolution paragraph.** Under page pressure it is the first casualty, and it is the one place you get to write the reviewer's summary for them. End every major section, and the Research Strategy as a whole, with a paragraph that reiterates the big issue and explains how the pieces address it. A reviewer's opinion often does not solidify until the end. End strong.
- **Hype destroys credibility.** Boldface, buzzwords, and superlatives read as a substitute for substance. Concrete beats emphatic.
- **A proposal is a plan, not a promise.** Reviewers know no plan survives contact with the data. What they assess is whether you can adapt — so potential problems and alternative strategies are not a weakness to be minimized. Air the dirty laundry up front.
- **There is no revise-and-resubmit dialogue.** The proposal is judged once, as submitted, by readers whose job is to advise the agency rather than improve your work.
- **"Very Good" is functionally a rejection.** Far more proposals are fundable than fundable proposals get funded. Neutral enthusiasm loses. Faint praise in a summary statement is a worse sign than sharp criticism: criticism means they engaged.

## 5. Research Strategy

### Significance

- Establish the **scientific premise**: the prior published work and preliminary data the proposal rests on, *including their strengths and weaknesses*. This is required, and reviewers look for it. Do not present the literature as uniformly solid.
- Explain what changes if the work succeeds — for the field, for practice, for the institute's mission.
- Do not restate the aims page. Deepen it.

### Innovation

Under the SRF this is read as part of importance. State plainly what is new: a concept, a method, a model system, an analytic approach, a combination not previously applied to this problem. Distinguish *new to the field* from *new to me*. Novelty without importance scores badly.

### Approach

This is Factor 2 and it is scored on its own. Give it the space.

For each aim, in the same order every time:

1. **Rationale and working hypothesis.**
2. **Design** — including the comparison, the unit of analysis, and randomization or sampling.
3. **Methods** — enough detail that a reviewer can judge competence, not a protocol.
4. **Rigor safeguards** — blinding, randomization, pre-specified outcomes, replication, and the strategy for unbiased analysis.
5. **Relevant biological variables** — how sex is factored into design and analysis for vertebrate animal and human studies; also age, weight, and disease state where relevant. Studying only one sex requires strong justification.
6. **Statistical analysis and power** — the specific test for the specific question, effect size assumed, and the source of that assumption.
7. **Expected outcomes.**
8. **Potential problems and alternative strategies** — real risks, real alternatives. This section is where feasibility is won.
9. **Timeline and milestones.**

Include preliminary data that establishes feasibility and supports the premise. Insufficient detail and insufficient preliminary data are two of the most common fixable reasons for a poor score.

### Where the rigor elements go

| Element | Location |
|---|---|
| Scientific premise (strengths and weaknesses of prior work) | Significance |
| Rigorous experimental design; unbiased results | Approach |
| Relevant biological variables, including sex | Approach |
| Authentication of key biological/chemical resources | **Separate attachment**, not the Research Strategy — and it does not count against the page limit |

The authentication attachment covers authentication and validation only. Methods and preliminary data belong in the Research Strategy. K02, K05, and K24 are exempt from these rigor requirements.

## 6. K awards

The research plan and the career plan must be one argument: *this specific science, in this specific environment, with these specific mentors, is what turns me into an independent investigator.*

**Candidate.** Show trajectory, not a CV in prose. If the publication record is thin, explain your actual role in the work rather than leaving reviewers to guess. Name the gap in your own training that the award closes.

**Career development plan.** The most common weakness is failing to justify why the K is *essential* to reaching independence. Be concrete: coursework with names and terms, structured mentorship with meeting frequency, specific skills and the specific experiments that require them, defined milestones by year, and an explicit plan for monitoring and evaluating progress. Tie the endpoint to a named next step — usually an R01 with a stated topic.

**Research plan.** Do not shrink it. Reviewers examine it closely and expect scope appropriate to a mentored stage: ambitious enough to matter, contained enough to finish. Do not reuse the mentor's human subjects section; describe the work *you* will do under the award. Justify sample size against the scale of a K, not an R01.

**Mentors.** A terse or generic mentor letter signals low engagement and reviewers read it that way. Each mentor statement should show a track record of producing independent investigators, current funding, a specific role in this plan, and a monitoring schedule. Distinguish primary mentor, co-mentor, and consultants by what each contributes.

**Environment and institutional commitment.** Protected time in writing, named resources, and evidence that the candidate will be integral to the institution's research program — not merely accommodated.

## 7. Other components

- **Project Summary/Abstract (30 lines).** Self-contained, written for a broad scientific audience, no citations. Problem, gap, objective, aims in one clause each, and impact. Written last.
- **Project Narrative (3 sentences).** Public-health relevance in plain language.
- **Resubmissions.** One page introducing the resubmission. Answer every substantive critique, group responses by theme rather than reviewer, and mark changes in the body as the NOFO directs. Do not argue; concede what is fair and show what changed. If you disagree, say so once, with evidence, and move on.
- **Cover letter.** Request the study section and institute. Name conflicts. Keep to one page.
- **Letters of support** must say something specific about this project. Generic enthusiasm is noise.

## 8. Pre-submission checklist

**Story**

1. Can the aims page be read alone and understood by an educated non-expert?
2. Is the gap stated explicitly, in words that signal a gap?
3. Is the central hypothesis falsifiable and testable with the proposed methods?
4. Are the aims independent, active-verb, and free of concepts introduced nowhere else?
5. Does the last paragraph return to the first paragraph's problem?
6. Is the whole story on the page by the end of page two?

**Score**

7. Does the application make an explicit case for importance (Factor 1), separately from feasibility?
8. Does every aim in the Approach carry rigor safeguards, a power justification, expected outcomes, and alternatives?
9. Are scientific premise, biological variables, and authentication each in the right place?
10. Would a reviewer marking Factor 3 find any gap in expertise or resources? If so, has a collaborator been added to close it?

**Craft**

11. Does each section end with a resolution paragraph that a reviewer could paste into a summary statement?
12. Is anything cut to fit the page limit still in the margins or the font size? Fix by condensing prose instead.
13. Any hype, any boldface substituting for argument, any superlative unsupported by data?
14. Read aloud: does the pacing hold?

**Compliance**

15. Page limits, per the NOFO rather than per habit.
16. Application count for the calendar year within the six-application cap (T and R13 excluded).
17. Common Form biosketch generated in SciENcv, ORCID linked, personal statement within 3,500 characters.
18. DMS Plan on the current format, Element 4 under 300 words.
19. Every reference verified — real, correctly attributed, and actually saying what it is cited for.

## 9. Common reasons applications score poorly

Fixable, per NIAID: poor presentation and writing; insufficient experimental detail; insufficient preliminary data; failure to convince reviewers of significance to public health and the institute's mission; a team missing needed expertise; inadequate discussion of obstacles and alternatives.

Hard to fix: a low-impact topic; an unsound hypothesis or one unsupported by data; methods that cannot test the hypothesis; work that duplicates existing research; no reviewer enthusiasm for the direction.

K-specific, per NIAID: no clear justification for why the award is essential to independence; an underdeveloped research plan; a human subjects section lifted from the mentor's grant; lukewarm mentor letters; a thin publication record with no explanation of the candidate's role; sample size unjustified for a K-scale project.
